2. ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Distinguishing epistemic uncertainty (model ignorance) from aleatoric uncertainty
(task randomness) is critical for reliable AI systems, yet standard confidence
evaluation metrics capture different and incomplete aspects of uncertainty. While
AUC and accuracy measure predictive signal, proper scoring rules assess overall
uncertainty, and calibration metrics isolate part of the epistemic uncertainty but
ignore within-bin heterogeneity of errors, known as grouping loss. We bridge
this evaluation gap by introducing asymptotically consistent and sample-efficient
estimators of the grouping loss and excess decision risk, providing a fine-grained
assessment of epistemic uncertainty that complements existing calibration metrics.
Applied to LLM question-answering with inherent aleatoric noise, our estimators
reveal substantial grouping loss which decreases with model scale and instruction
tuning. Their local nature enables automatic identification of subgroups with
systematic over- or under-confidence, supporting interpretable confidence audits.
Finally, we leverage these estimates to design LLM cascades that defer high excess
decision risk predictions to stronger models, achieving higher accuracy at lower
cost than competing approaches.

3. METHODOLOGY SUMMARY
================================================================================

CORE PROBLEM:
Standard calibration metrics (like ECE) only capture part of epistemic uncertainty.
A model can be well-calibrated on average but systematically overconfident on some
subgroups and underconfident on others -- the two effects cancel out. The authors
call this hidden heterogeneity "grouping loss" (GL).

PROPER SCORING RULE DECOMPOSITION (Theorem 1):
Expected Loss = Calibration Loss (CL) + Grouping Loss (GL) + Aleatoric Loss
Where Epistemic Loss = CL + GL.
Key insight: calibration alone is an incomplete picture of epistemic error.

GROUPING LOSS DEFINITION:
GL = E[(f*(X) - c o f(X))^2] = E_p[V[f*(X) | f(X) = p]]
This measures the variance of true probabilities within a level set of the model f.
A model that assigns the same confidence to inputs with different true probabilities
has high grouping loss.

THEIR ESTIMATORS:

(a) Grouping Loss Estimator (GL_lb):
- Uses partition-based estimation instead of traditional binning
- Builds an "honest regression tree" (Wager & Athey, 2017) on the residuals
  R = Y - c o f(X), where c is the calibration function (Platt scaling)
- The tree partitions the input space to minimize intra-leaf variance of residuals
- Debiased estimator: GL_lb = sum_j p_hat_j * (r_hat_j^2 - v_hat_j / n_j)
- Asymptotically consistent: converges to true GL as sample size increases
- More sample-efficient than prior binning-based methods (Perez-Lebel et al., 2023)

(b) Pointwise Excess Decision Risk Estimator (R_hat):
- Estimates per-sample epistemic decision risk
- R_f(x) = Lambda_Delta * |f*(X) - t*| when model and oracle disagree, else 0
- Uses the same partition-based residual estimate to approximate f*(X)
- Key property: POINTWISE -- gives a risk score for each individual input
- This is what enables the LLM cascade application

PARTITION CONSTRUCTION:
- Honest regression trees: split data into fitting set (40%) and evaluation set (50%)
  plus calibration set (10%)
- Tree structure learned on fitting set, leaf estimates computed on evaluation set
- This prevents overfitting and provides unbiased leaf estimates
- Unlimited depth, minimum 15 samples per leaf
- For LLMs: partition built on query features or embeddings

CALIBRATION:
- Platt scaling used for calibration throughout
- Calibration is applied first, then residuals are analyzed for grouping loss

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP:

(a) Semi-synthetic validation (Section 4.1):
- Weather dataset from TabReD (16M+ samples)
- Gradient Boosted tree for confidence scores
- Validates that GL_lb converges to true GL

(b) LLM Grouping Loss Audit (Section 4.2):
- Folktexts benchmark (Cruz et al., 2024): tabular data converted to natural
  language questions with binary-choice answers
- 5 ACS datasets: ACSIncome, ACSEmployment, ACSMobility, ACSTravelTime,
  ACSPublicCoverage
- Tasks have inherent aleatoric uncertainty (outcome can't be determined from input)
- 27 open-source LLMs, 1B to 70B parameters
- Confidence scores from next-token logprobs: P(yes) / (P(yes) + P(no))
- Zero-shot evaluation

(c) LLM Cascades (Section 4.3):
- Cascade pool: Llama 3 instruction-tuned family (1B, 3B, 8B, 70B), Phi4 (14B),
  Gemma 2 (27B), Mixtral 8x7B
- 120 different cascade pool configurations across 5 datasets
- Compared against: predictive router (Hu et al., 2024), calibration-only cascade,
  and biggest-model baseline

================================================================================
4. KEY FINDINGS / RESULTS
================================================================================

GROUPING LOSS IN LLMS:
- LLMs exhibit substantial grouping loss (GL), meaning their confidence scores
  hide significant within-bin heterogeneity
- GL decreases with model size (clear size effect across 1B to 70B)
- Instruction-tuned models have lower (or equal) GL compared to base models
- Instruction tuning polarizes answer distributions and induces overconfidence
  (confirming Cruz et al., 2024) but reduces subgroup-specific biases
- Even after calibration, systematic biases remain for demographic subgroups
  (e.g., experienced graduate women's income underestimated)

ESTIMATOR QUALITY:
- Their GL_lb estimator converges to true GL from below (lower bound)
- More sample-efficient than Perez-Lebel et al. (2023) binning-based method
- Binning-free approach captures more heterogeneity in confidence scores

LLM CASCADES:
- Their epistemic risk cascade outperforms the largest model in the pool at a
  fraction of its cost: on average +6% accuracy gain at 46% of cost
- Accounting for full epistemic risk (CL + GL) vs. calibration-only (CL) yields
  up to 15% accuracy improvement on high-GL datasets like ACSEmployment
- Outperforms predictive router baseline (Hu et al., 2024) in 60% of cases,
  achieving strict Pareto optimality (higher accuracy at lower cost)
- Robust to training set size: with 6,000+ samples, performance is consistent;
  even with 2,000 samples, +12% accuracy on high-GL datasets
- At high willingness-to-pay: +4% accuracy over Llama 70B at ~30% of its cost

SUBGROUP IDENTIFICATION:
- The tree-based partition automatically identifies over/under-confident subgroups
- Example: Llama 3.3 70B Instruct on ACSIncome:
  * Group 37 (young graduate women, age <= 24): underconfident
  * Group 62 (experienced graduate women, professional degree, age >= 37): overconfident
- These local biases are invisible to global calibration metrics

5. RELEVANCE TO OUR WORK (Bayesian UQ Project)
================================================================================

--- UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION ---

HIGH RELEVANCE. This paper directly addresses the gap between calibration and full
epistemic uncertainty. Their key concept:

"A classifier may be well-calibrated overall yet overconfident on some subgroups
and underconfident on others, with the two effects canceling out."

This maps directly to our finding about "fragile confidence" -- high single-prompt
confidence + low cross-permutation consistency. Their grouping loss is essentially
measuring the same phenomenon we detect through permutation disagreement: the model
assigns similar confidence to inputs where it should be more or less certain.

Their decomposition: Epistemic Loss = Calibration Loss + Grouping Loss
Our finding: Per-query MSP ~0.91, ECE = 0.186, but mean across permutations is
well-calibrated. The per-query overconfidence we observe IS grouping loss in their
framework -- the model assigns high confidence to inputs that actually have different
true probabilities.

--- LOGPROB ANALYSIS ---

MODERATE RELEVANCE. They extract confidence scores from next-token logprobs:
P(yes) / (P(yes) + P(no)) for binary-choice questions. This is analogous to our
approach of extracting [P(A), P(B), P(C), P(D)] from first-token logprobs.

Key difference: they use binary outcomes (yes/no), we use 4-way MCQ. They don't
do multiple queries per input -- they get one confidence score per question.

They observe the same overconfidence phenomenon we do: instruction-tuned models
produce overconfident logprobs. Their solution is Platt scaling (post-hoc
recalibration); our solution is temperature scaling T=3.0 on the likelihood
aggregation.

--- ANSWER SHUFFLING / PERMUTATION ---

NOT ADDRESSED. This paper does NOT use answer shuffling, permutation testing,
or multiple queries per input. They query each LLM once per question and analyze
the resulting confidence score. Their approach to measuring epistemic uncertainty
is entirely different from ours:
- OURS: Present the same question N times with shuffled answers, observe variance
  in logprob vectors across permutations as a signal of epistemic uncertainty
- THEIRS: Use a single confidence score per question, then use a learned partition
  (honest tree) on input features to detect where confidence is miscalibrated

This is a fundamentally different paradigm. We create artificial perturbations to
probe uncertainty; they analyze the geometry of the confidence-vs-outcome space.

--- BAYESIAN AGGREGATION ---

NOT ADDRESSED. They do not aggregate multiple probability vectors. No Bayesian
posterior, no product-of-experts, no Dirichlet aggregation. Their framework is
frequentist: partition-based estimation with honest regression trees.

--- ADAPTIVE STOPPING ---

NOT DIRECTLY ADDRESSED, but their cascade framework is conceptually parallel.
Their cascade: "query cheap model first, defer to expensive model if epistemic
risk is high." This is analogous to our adaptive stopping: "query with few
permutations first, do more if posterior is uncertain, escalate to think mode
if still uncertain."

Key parallel:
- OURS: tau threshold on posterior confidence -> stop early or escalate
- THEIRS: tau threshold on epistemic risk estimate -> keep cheap model or defer
  to expensive model

Their cascade achieves: cheaper compute by routing easy questions to small models
Our adaptive stopping achieves: cheaper compute by stopping early on easy questions

Both are fundamentally about "spend compute where uncertainty is high."

--- CALIBRATION ---

HIGH RELEVANCE. Their entire framework is built on the insight that calibration
is necessary but insufficient. Specific connections:

1. They use Platt scaling for calibration. We use temperature scaling (T=3.0).
   Both are post-hoc recalibration methods addressing overconfident logprobs.

2. Their grouping loss measures "hidden miscalibration within calibration bins."
   Our permutation-based variance measures something similar: even after
   temperature scaling makes the aggregate well-calibrated, individual queries
   show high variance, indicating the model's confidence is fragile.

3. Their finding that "instruction-tuning polarizes answer distributions" maps
   to our finding about CoT/think mode collapsing logprobs (scaffolding absorption).

4. ECE limitations: They explicitly argue ECE misses grouping loss. We found
   ECE = 0.186 per-query but good calibration after aggregation. Both papers
   show ECE alone is misleading.

--- DECISION DEFERRAL / ROUTING ---

HIGH RELEVANCE. Their LLM cascade is directly comparable to our routing framework:
- They route between different-sized models (1B -> 3B -> 8B -> 70B)
- We route between different inference modes (direct -> CoT -> think)
- Both use uncertainty estimates to decide when to "spend more compute"
- Their metric: pointwise excess decision risk estimate
- Our metric: posterior confidence after N permutations

Their cascade result (+6% accuracy at 46% cost) is a useful comparison point for
our adaptive stopping results (avg_N=2.76 of 10, accuracy maintained, 15% escalated).
